The Painter’s Sanctuary: Ramón Gaya in Rome

Gigliopad isn’t just a rental; it’s a living slice of European cultural history. This building on Vicolo del Giglio was the longtime home and studio of Ramón Gaya (1910–2005), one of Spain’s most celebrated painters, essayists, and intellectuals. A key figure of the Generation of '27 and the cultural missions of the Second Spanish Republic—where he traveled across Spain bringing reproductions of the Prado's masterpieces to remote villages—Gaya was forced into exile following the Spanish Civil War and the tragic death of his wife during the 1939 bombing of Figueres.

After periods of exile in France and Mexico, Gaya returned to Europe in the 1950s and discovered in Rome his true spiritual home. He chose this quiet, luminous corner of Rione Regola. For decades, bathed in the gentle Roman light from the windows overlooking the terracotta roofs between Piazza Farnese and Campo de' Fiori, Gaya painted his luminous still lifes, Roman landscapes, and revered homage portraits to Velázquez, Titian, and Rembrandt. It was here that he wrote his seminal philosophical work, El sentimiento de la pintura (The Sentiment of Painting). In 2002, he was awarded Spain’s prestigious inaugural Velázquez Prize for Plastic Arts.

The House of Farnese

The grandeur of Regola was forged by the Farnese dynasty—one of the most powerful and shrewd families of Renaissance Italy. Originally provincial military lords from Lazio, their ascent accelerated when Giulia Farnese caught the favor of Pope Alexander VI (Borgia), and her brother Alessandro became Cardinal. In 1534, Alessandro was crowned Pope Paul III, initiating a historic sixteen-year papacy that shaped the Counter-Reformation: he convened the Council of Trent, recognized the Jesuit Order, commissioned Michelangelo for The Last Judgment, and established the hereditary Duchy of Parma and Piacenza for his son Pier Luigi.

Construction of Palazzo Farnese (now the French Embassy) began in 1517 under Antonio da Sangallo the Younger and was dramatically expanded following Paul III’s papal election. Upon Sangallo’s death in 1546, Paul III appointed Michelangelo Buonarroti to complete the palace.

Michelangelo’s architectural signatures define the building to this day:

• The Monumental Cornice: Michelangelo completely redesigned the massive, deeply projecting classical cornice that crowns the top of the building, creating one of the most celebrated rooflines in the world.
• The Central Balcony & Papal Crest: He redesigned the central upper-story window and balcony above the main portal, crowning it with the colossal coat of arms of Pope Paul III.
• The Courtyard Upper Level: He added and refined the soaring third story of the inner courtyard.
• The Tiber Bridge: Michelangelo conceived a private bridge over the Tiber to connect Palazzo Farnese with Villa Farnesina in Trastevere; the beloved Arco Farnese over Via Giulia stands today as the bridge's initial span.

What is a fleur-de-lis?

Fleur-de-lis literally means "lily flower" in French.

You will find many fleur-de-lis symbols at and around the nearby Palazzo Farnese, as the Farnese family used the fleur-de-lis as their primary heraldic symbol (representing irises/lilies). As with the other craft streets in the neighborhood, Vicolo del Giglio attained its name from this enduring symbol and the presence of local florists.

Secret Regola & Ancient Echoes

Beyond the grand palazzos lie the hidden layers of Roman history. Seek out the Arco degli Acetari (Arch of the Vinegar Makers) off Via del Pellegrino, a secluded medieval courtyard of rustic terracotta homes that feels completely frozen in the year 1600.

For a deeper descent into history, visit the Stadium of Domitian beneath Piazza Navona to see the ancient foundations of the city.

It’s said that the feral cats you can find lounging in the ruins at the Largo di Torre Argentina are descended from the cats Cleopatra gave to Antony. That’s not actually true, but you can visit the place where Julius Caesar was stabbed to death, at the Curia of Pompey, just outside Pompey’s Theater, roughly somewhere around the bus stop on Via Torre di Argentina, right in front of Come ‘na Vorta (tourist trap). And you can find pillars from Pompey’s Theater as spolia in the facade of Phot Lui, a clothing store on Via dei Giubbonari (jacket street), just a block from the apartment.
